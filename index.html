Matthew
Cissna

copper leaves, a lost mitten, and the plan you keep meaning to throw away

Foreword

I wrote most of these looking backward and they aren't really about missing being a kid, they're more about things that you figure out when you're little, but don't really know how to process.

So these are childhood poems, but they aren't necessarily very soft. A few of them are small and quiet, a couple reach past my own childhood toward other people's. The last one, "Advice to Myself," is a poem that looks ahead, instead of back.

Make of them what you will.

I

Emperor Butterflies Dancing Under Moonlight

sijo
Black and white spots paint fiery amber wings that singe your fingertips. They waltz in seas of twilight like seeds dropping from maples. Forever nestled in a cocoon they may only dream.
II

lost mitten

sijo
A red mitten on the fence post, thumb pointed at the sky, waits out the week, then the month, fading toward a polite pink. Somewhere a small bare hand has already forgotten it was warm.
III

Echo

Shout your name down the stone well, and the well says it back, smaller. It hands you a thinner version, a sort of one that fits before you grow. Children learn this game for free… the world will answer, but charges interest.
IV

For When I Become a Cowboy

When I become a Cowboy, I'll ride until the burnt copper sun bleeds into the mesa, I'll chase the horizon where the wild wind blows through canyon bones, and pluck my banjo with calloused, sun-cracked hands, a toothpick might be sitting on the crease of my lips. When I become a cowboy, I'll lay my brim down low over my tired eyes and perch back in a hickory rocking chair sipping something smoky and sharp I'll keep my eyes low and let it sting my throat.
V

Skunk Cabbage

Skunk cabbage rotting into the soil. didn't expect it, far from anything dead. It gets past the cold. The smell of this place isn't a smell so much as a temperature the nose forced to translate. Alder bark in November, a particular gray no paint company bothers to name, holds something the rain is pulling out of it, slowly, like a bruise surfacing three days after. Always underneath something else, fungal, old, the accurate word lives in a genus, Fomitopsis, or close, and still it rises through the duff, through the ferns gone soft since September, through what was a nurse log last year and this year is becoming the soil beneath it, the smell seeping into the back of your throat, before your nose registers.
VI

leaf day

clutching my hot cocoa in my favorite metal mug the cold found the tight gap at my collar, wet leaves covered the street, little me lapping around the yard in my sweater, my little stained dungarees, knit beanie hat made by my mother and blue and green nikes that lit when I stomped, bundled up before school, when the yellow trucks groaned in, and sucked up all the copper rusted leaves, oh, i was overjoyed then.
VII

On the Jetty

My father walked the black rocks out farther than I would follow. The signs all said people die here, turned-around waves, riptide currents, sneaker waves. My father walked. The black rocks out. Farther than I would follow. I stayed where the sand went hard and dark, the line the tide had quit converging on, and watched his back get small and casual, a man who'd read the same signs. My father walked. The black rocks out. Farther than I would follow. The gulls were loud about nothing. I did a kid's bad math about how far I'd have to go to yell, and how I wouldn't be able to. Nobody in sight, except a scared small boy. My father walked. The black rocks out. Farther than I would follow. He came back fine he always came back, that was the whole trick of him, and I learned the wait before the relief, which is the part I still know. My father walked. The black rocks out. Farther than I would follow.
VIII

I asked for a muffin

spoken word
I asked for a muffin you gave me a scone. A little sad pastry, on a plate of its own. I asked for the summer, You gave me the rain. I asked for a nickel, you gave me a dime. I asked for my father, and you gave me his coat. A long, empty sleeve, with an unfinished note. I asked for the city, you gave me the block, I asked for a minute, you handed me a clock. I asked for forever, you gave me a while. I asked for an answer, You gave me a smile. I learned how to hold what the world let me keep. I ate the whole thing and walked myself home.
IX

Zoo

The penguins smelled like a wet basement and nobody had warned me. I'd wanted them since the bus ride and now I wanted to go home. There was a keeper hosing the rocks in tall green rubber boots. The others bunched near the cold spray shoving for the wet spots, but one stood off on the dry shelf facing the painted wall. I asked what's wrong with it nothing is, the others pick one every few years and that one's the one they picked. No reason he could point to it just got chosen out. It wasn't sick or dying it ate when they threw the fish. It just stood where the others weren't it didn't agree to the terms. I was small and the word for it came later, the playground word, and later again the grown-up word again it happens in the lunch table, the group text. But the bird is where I first saw it done cleanly, without a reason, one of them set slightly apart and everyone fine with it.
X

What He Carried

spoken word
There were no great truths at the border. No one handed him a country in his mouth. There was a folding chair, and a number too. The man behind the pass-through window didn't look up. Glass like a ticket booth, except for what it sold. Oh, he decided things. He had a bag. In the bag: a phone with a cracked face, a charger for a wall that no longer existed, And there was a key. He kept the key not for a lock on a door for the hand that used to turn it. A photograph gone soft at the corners from the hands that kept reaching for it. He is not a story about courage. He didn't want to be brave. He just did not want to die in the place he was from, which is a smaller, more ordinary thing. He shouldn't have had to choose it. So now he buses tables in a town he can't spell. Now he stands in a lot at six in the morning with other men who don't talk about it. He learns the word appointment. He learns the word pending. He knows the word patience, for it is all he ever knew, which the woman at the desk uses as if it costs her nothing. Some sit in the chair. And wait. And are told to come back later. And come back later. And sit in the chair. Some of them get sent home to what they fled from. Some don't. There is no lesson in him. He bent down this morning and went to work. The key is still in his pocket. He can feel it when he kneels.
XI

Advice to Myself

Don't write up the future. When your great-uncle asks, "What's your five-year plan?" leave it unmade, in the drawer where you drafted it, let the ink go gray, and let the bullet points wilt like celery in the bottom drawer of the fridge. Let the resume yellow, let the water stains make it impossible to read. Don't update it. Don't polish the verbs of quantifying the summers, that you could've spent living out your youth. Let the calendar fill with white space. Don't colour-code the hours, or optimize your meetings. Throw out the backup plan, don't draft another. Don't hedge every bet. Let in a little risk sometimes, let the spreadsheet do the worrying, and prove its math wrong anyway. The best things are never in the projections. Pursue the authentic. But first, decide what is authentic, and then go after it with all your heart. Don't sort the maybes from the certains. Don't worry if you're eating cereal for dinner at twenty-two. Accept new forms of life. Let the strangers in through the screen door, for a meal, and memories. Read nothing except what tears down the insulation between yourself and your one life, and what shatters this ruse you keep calling the plan. return to top ↑
